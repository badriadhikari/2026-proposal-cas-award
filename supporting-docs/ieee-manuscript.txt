\begin{abstract}

\textit{Contribution;} This paper introduces an instructional approach \reviewchange{in which students reflect on coding process reports generated by} a web-based process-tracking compiler\reviewchange{, and reports students' perceptions of this approach.}
\textit{Background;} As students learn to code, they \reviewchange{develop habits, such as} over-relying on Generative AI tools or brute-forcing code until it works. \reviewchange{Detecting these habits early} can help students refine their learning strategies. \reviewchange{The Process Feedback compiler records students' coding process and turns it into a report with quantitative summaries and visual charts that are designed to support self-reflection.}
\textit{Research Questions;} 1) How can process-tracking online compilers be used in \reviewchange{CS1 and CS2 classrooms to support student self-reflection}? 2) \reviewchange{How do students perceive the helpfulness of a process-tracking online compiler for their learning}? 3) How helpful \reviewchange{do students find} coding process reports for completing reflection activities?
\textit{Methodology;} \reviewchange{A case study at a U.S. public university examined students' perceptions of this reflective approach. Students in CS1 and CS2 used the compiler for all coursework during one semester and were offered an end-of-term bonus reflection assignment with ten open-ended questions. Of 111 invited students, 59 completed a survey.}
\textit{Findings;} Students responded positively to \reviewchange{the tool and the coding process reports. Of the students who responded, 79.3\%} rated Process Feedback as helpful for their learning, and 74\% of those who completed the reflection assignment found the \reviewchange{reports} helpful for reviewing their work, recognizing syntax flaws, and identifying outside resource dependencies.

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{S}{tudents} learning to code often face several challenges. Many become frustrated when confronted with cryptic compiler error messages. Others feel discouraged when they realize that instructors
tend to focus on the final code submission rather than the effort and process behind it. In addition, many students view programming as a narrow technical skill rather than a practice that fosters broader problem solving and reflective thinking. At the same time, instructors often lack the time or tools to understand the variety
of challenges their students face. These issues highlight the need to shift the focus from the end product to the learning process. By making students’ coding processes visible, programming instructors \reviewchange{may} support meaningful reflection and \reviewchange{encourage} metacognitive development. This paper explores how such process-oriented approaches can be integrated into programming education and how students perceive and respond to the practice of reflecting on their
own coding behavior. \reviewchange{The Process Feedback compiler records each student's coding process, and students then reflect on the resulting report \mbox{(see Fig.~\ref{teaser})}. This reflection is expected to support learning. This study reports students' perceptions of this approach.} This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It describes the features of the Process Feedback online coding application \cite{adhikari2023}, \reviewchange{explaining how it makes} students' coding processes visible and \reviewchange{enables} data-driven reflection.
    \item It presents students' perceptions of using a process-logging tool for everyday programming assignments, and their experiences of reflecting on their own coding practices.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Pensieve \cite{yan2019}, a coding process logging and visualization tool similar to Process Feedback, was developed and tested in 2019. Its approach is to “auto-save hundreds of intermediate process steps into a repository,” allowing students to submit this data along with their final answer so that “the teaching assistants could spend 5 to 10 minutes observing the student’s process.” The two primary objectives behind Pensieve’s design were to foster metacognitive skills in students and to counteract plagiarism. Its pedagogical foundation is that metacognitive practices represent some of the highest-level cognitive skills in the Revised Bloom’s Taxonomy for learning \cite{conklin2005}, and these practices directly contribute to improved student performance \cite{fuller2007}. During small group instructional diagnosis, teaching assistants noted that using Pensieve required additional effort. However, both students and instructors reported overwhelmingly positive perceptions of the tool. Similar to Pensieve, numerous integrated development environment (IDE) plugins and tools have been developed to collect data at
various levels of granularity \cite{edwards2023}. Examples include the OSBIDE plugin for Microsoft Visual Studio \cite{carter2017}, the DevEventTracker plugin for Eclipse \cite{kazerouni2017}, and the ShowYourWork plugin for JetBrains IDE \cite{edwards2022}. It is worth noting that process-oriented approaches extend beyond
programming and are applicable in other domains of learning. For instance, tools such as InputLog \cite{vandermeulen2023}, which track students’ writing processes, have been shown to be effective in improving writing skills through self-reflection exercises.

\reviewchange{Reflection in programming education is closely tied to metacognition and self-regulated learning, in which learners plan their approach, monitor their progress, and evaluate and adapt their strategies \mbox{\cite{zimmerman2002}}. Novice programmers often struggle with these skills \mbox{\cite{prather2018}}, which are best developed through explicit instruction \mbox{\cite{pintrich2002}}; novices who received explicit guidance on monitoring their problem-solving process showed higher productivity and self-efficacy \mbox{\cite{loksa2016chi}}. Coding process reports can provide such support by giving students concrete evidence of how they planned, implemented, revised, debugged, and completed their work.}

\section{Process Feedback Online Compiler}
Process Feedback, accessible at \href{https://processfeedback.org}{https://processfeedback.org}, supports C, C++, Python, and Java, and does not require students to create an account. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:tool_comparison}, Process Feedback provides unique features compared to other available tools. It also supports Python Turtle-based programming. While students are coding, their process data is logged locally in their browser’s storage on their own device. The online compiler does not track user behavior using analytics tools such as Google Analytics; thus, students are not actively monitored. Process Feedback builds upon tools such as \reviewchange{Pensieve} and ShowYourWork, offering enhanced functionality and usability. At any point during the coding process (see Figure~\ref{main-figure}), students can switch from the code editor to a dedicated interface for viewing and exploring their coding process. This student-facing design shifts the responsibility for reviewing the coding process primarily to students themselves, rather than to teaching assistants or instructors. Instead of requiring instructors to review each student’s coding history, this approach to process transparency encourages students to examine their own habits and reflect on them independently.

\subsection{Process Report PDF}

Although many institutions rely on automated grading systems, those with smaller class sizes often either cannot afford these paid tools or prefer human grading for pedagogical reasons. In such settings, students typically submit their coding assignments as multiple files, which may include source code, screenshots of program output, and screenshots of input provided to the code. These files are submitted either individually as attachments or bundled into a single compressed zip file. During grading, instructors or teaching assistants must review each submitted file and execute the code separately to verify its correctness. This process of reviewing individual files and running student-submitted code can be time-consuming and significantly increase the grading workload.

Process Feedback allows students to download a coding process report as a single PDF that can be shared with others. This PDF includes all relevant details that an instructor or  grader may need to assess the assignment, such as the final code, any inputs provided to the program, and the most recent code execution output. In addition, the report includes process summaries and visualizations, such as total time spent coding, charts indicating breaks, copy-paste events, AI usage history, and execution history. The PDF also provides a link to an interactive version of the report, enabling instructors or graders to further explore the student’s coding process and even resume coding from where the student left off.

\subsection{Explanatory AI as an Assistant}
Amidst the growing enthusiasm for integrating AI into coding education, recent research suggests that allowing students to interact
freely with a large language model while coding may actually reduce engagement \cite{nie2024}. However, several other studies suggest that
students are more receptive when AI is used specifically as an explanatory or feedback tool \cite{ghimire2024}. For instance, in a study involving 76
students who received automated feedback while learning to code,
82\% reported that the system helped them develop good coding
practices \cite{saliba2024}. Similarly, a survey of approximately 700 students at
Harvard University found that overall perceptions were positive,
with some students describing the experience as having “a personal
tutor” \cite{liu2024}.
These findings suggest that many students find it helpful when
AI explains compiler error messages in clear, understandable language. Process Feedback incorporates this explanatory use of generative AI through two features: one that explains selected portions of
a student’s code and the other that clarifies compiler error messages.
In a separate multi-country study involving around 200 students
using Process Feedback, CS1 students expressed appreciation for
these AI-driven explanations \cite{adhikari2024fie}.

\subsection{\reviewheading{Academic Integrity in Introductory Programming}}

Process-oriented coding tools such as Process Feedback \reviewchange{may offer} a novel
approach to promoting academic integrity. Traditional plagiarism
detection tools often fall short when applied to code submissions
\cite{bommasani2021}, making it difficult to assess the integrity of student work in
programming courses. Furthermore, instances of misconduct are
typically identified only after the work has been submitted, at
which point students are more likely to defend their actions than
to acknowledge any wrongdoing.
Compounding the issue, many educational tools provide instructors with access to students’ “behavior analysis data” without students being fully aware of what is being collected. When such
tools reveal potential misconduct, they can trigger a confrontational process between the instructor and the student, which can
be uncomfortable or even intimidating for both parties.
An ideal approach to academic integrity is to discourage plagiarism before it occurs. In this context, emphasizing students’ coding
processes \reviewchange{may help reduce} integrity issues in \reviewchange{introductory programming} courses. A process-focused approach \reviewchange{can be} beneficial at
all three stages of an assignment: (1) before students begin, it encourages them to reflect on how they plan to approach the task; (2)
during the assignment, it reminds them that the goal is to improve
their process, not just to produce a correct outcome; and (3) after
submission, it provides instructors with ample data to detect and
discuss potential misconduct as well as to recognize students’ effort
and improvement \reviewchange{\mbox{(see Figure~\ref{main-figure})}}. \reviewchange{Process data alone cannot establish a student's intent or prove misconduct, but it can open a conversation with the student.}


\section{Case Study and Student Perception}

\reviewchange{Students in CS1 and CS2 courses at a public university in the United States engaged with Process Feedback in three ways: (1) throughout the semester, they completed all assignments in the online compiler and submitted a coding process report with each assignment; (2) toward the end of the semester, they were offered a bonus reflection assignment asking them to reflect on their own reports; and (3) during finals week, they were invited to complete a voluntary survey about their experience with the tool and the reflection assignment.} They were informed at the beginning of the course that submitting their coding process would be required.

\subsection{\reviewheading{Course Context}}

\reviewchange{Both courses were taught in C++ over a 14-week semester, and four instructors taught the four sections using the same approach. Assignments consisted of homework and projects, and each coding process report was submitted as a PDF through the learning management system. Instructors graded assignments by hand, without an autograder, reviewing both the final code and the process data in each report. The tool, the report requirement, and the end-of-term reflection assignment were the same in both courses, so CS2 students were included in the study.}

\subsection{\reviewheading{End-of-Semester Reflection Questions}}

\reviewchange{The bonus reflection assignment consisted of} ten reflection questions about \reviewchange{students'} coding process. These questions addressed topics such as time management, problem-solving strategies, and how students responded when they encountered difficulties. \reviewchange{Students were asked to use the online version of their coding process report and include dates, times, and examples from the report in their responses. The ten questions, grouped into four categories, are listed below.}

\subsubsection*{\reviewheading{Overall Coding Process}}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \reviewchange{How did you approach the task? Describe your first plan after reading the instructions and how it changed as you worked, for example whether you tried alternative solutions, sought help, or searched online.}
    \item \reviewchange{Recall a moment when you felt stuck or frustrated. What did you do to move forward, and what did that moment reveal about gaps in your knowledge or your habits as a problem solver?}
    \item \reviewchange{Looking at your timeline, was your time management effective? Consider how long you worked, when you took breaks, and whether those breaks helped you return with new ideas or broke your momentum.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{\reviewheading{Debugging and Testing Habits}}
\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{3}
    \item \reviewchange{How many times did you run your code, and how many runs ended in errors? Find your longest streak of failed runs in the report and describe what you were trying to fix.}
    \item \reviewchange{What was your most common debugging strategy (e.g., trial and error, print statements, reading error messages)? Was it effective, and is there evidence that you switched strategies when one wasn't working?}
    \item \reviewchange{Which types of errors did you run into most often? Do they point to a recurring misunderstanding, such as a repeated syntax issue or the same logic mistake?}
    \item \reviewchange{When and how did you check that your code was correct: did you plan test cases before coding, test as you went, or test only at the end? What might your approach have missed?}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{\reviewheading{AI Use}}
\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{7}
    \item \reviewchange{Which AI tools did you use, and for what purposes (e.g., planning, generating code, debugging, explaining concepts)? How did your use of AI compare with looking things up yourself?}
    \item \reviewchange{Describe a time when an AI tool gave you incorrect, confusing, or unhelpful output. How did you notice, and how did you check or correct it?}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{\reviewheading{Looking Ahead}}
\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{9}
    \item \reviewchange{If you were to do this task again, what would you change? Name one specific habit you would keep and one you would change, based on what your report showed.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Survey Design}

For this study, students were invited to voluntarily complete a survey during finals week at the end of the semester. The survey included the following items:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Did the use of the Process Feedback online compiler help you in your learning in the course? (Likert scale: 1 to 5)
\item What is the reason (or reasons) for your choice above? (Open-ended)
\item What is your comment to the course instructor or the department about using tools like Process Feedback in coding courses? (Open-ended)
\item Did you complete the reflection assignment? (Yes or No)
\item Did the coding process reports generated by Process Feedback help you in completing the reflection assignment? (Likert scale: 1 to 5)
\item Was the reflection assignment helpful for your learning? (Likert scale: 1 to 5)
\item What did you learn about your coding habits when reflecting on your coding process? (Open-ended)
\end{enumerate}

Students who indicated that they had completed the reflection assignment (by answering ``yes'' to Question 4) were presented with  additional questions about their reflection experience (Questions 5 through 7). \reviewchange{The three Likert-scale items capture distinct perceptions: acceptance of the tool (Question 1), perceived usefulness of the coding process reports for reflection (Question 5), and perceived learning benefit of the reflection assignment (Question 6).}

\subsection{\reviewheading{Qualitative Analysis}}

\reviewchange{Responses to the open-ended questions (Questions 2, 3, and 7) were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis. Two of the authors independently read all responses, assigned initial codes, and grouped the codes into themes that were derived from the data rather than from a predefined list. The two coders then met to compare their coding and resolved all disagreements through discussion until consensus was reached. Responses to Question 3 were also categorized by sentiment as positive, negative, neutral, or mixed. Representative quotes were selected to illustrate each theme.}

\subsection{Student Participants}

Two online sections of CS1 students (N = 65), one in-person section of CS1 (N = 21), and one in-person section of CS2 students (N = 25) were invited to participate in the online survey. Of the 111 students invited, 59 completed the survey: 51 from CS1 and 8 from CS2. Among the respondents, 42 identified as male, 15 as female, and 2 as non-binary.

\subsection{Survey Findings}

In response to the question of whether using Process Feedback supported their learning, \reviewchange{46 of the 58 students who answered (79.3\%; one respondent left Question 1 unanswered)} rated it as helpful (Likert scale \( \geq 4 \)) and only one student rated it as ``not helpful at all''. Of the 59 students who completed the survey, 50 students had also completed the reflection assignment. Among these, 74\% found the coding process report to be helpful (Likert scale \( \geq 4 \)), while five students found \reviewchange{it} not helpful. Similarly, 56\% of the students who completed the reflection related survey questions agreed that it was helpful for their learning (Likert scale \( \geq 4 \)). \reviewchange{\mbox{Table~\ref{tab:likert}} reports the full distribution of responses to these three questions.}

\subsubsection{Students' Comments on Helpfulness for Their Learning}

Analysis of student responses to the open-ended question about the helpfulness of Process Feedback (Question 2) revealed several key reasons for its perceived value. A textual analysis of these responses identified five emergent themes.

Foremost among the emergent themes was the tool's usefulness for debugging and understanding errors. Twenty-two students specifically praised the integration of explanatory AI, which helped them identify and understand issues in their code in real-time. As one student noted, ``It explained the errors.'' A second prominent theme was increased self-awareness through  coding process review. One student reflected, ``It helps me think back and remember what I did.'' The tool also contributed to students' confidence and independence, with many expressing a greater ability to handle coding challenges autonomously. For example, one student commented, ``It helped me learn and do different types of code myself.'' 

Ease of use emerged as another key theme, with 28 of 59 participants stating that they found the interface intuitive and beginner-friendly. Students' comments included, ``It is the best program to use for coding when you are starting as a beginner,'' and ``It was simple to use.'' Finally, several students noted that the tool encouraged them to critically evaluate their own work. One student stated: ``Yes, because it allowed me to review my work and look at my flaws.''

While the majority of feedback was positive, it is noteworthy that three out of \reviewchange{58} students rated Process Feedback as ``not helpful'' (Question 1)\reviewchange{, with one rating of 1 and two ratings of 2}. These students, and others, expressed various concerns. One remarked, ``Didn't help much but it is a good compiler.'' Five students noted the lack of advanced debugging features, and four found the interface less intuitive than other tools such as Visual Studio Code or PyCharm. One student wrote, ``Overall, it was okay. I really like PyCharm, though.'' In addition, two students were frustrated by frequent save reminders, with one commenting, ``The online compiler frequently interrupted my typing with save warnings.''

Analysis of responses to the open-ended question about students’ comments to the course instructor or the department (Question 3) revealed several key perceptions about the tool and its implementation. Student responses were categorized into four sentiment groups: positive, negative, neutral, and mixed (see Table \ref{tab:student_sentiment}).

Students praised Process Feedback as a ``great option for a coding course'' and a ``helpful tool,'' particularly for beginners. Many appreciated its web-based design, which eliminated the need for software installation, and it enabled code access across devices. Several students also \reviewchange{perceived it as a deterrent} to AI-assisted cheating, noting that it encouraged  them to type their own code. Additionally, students acknowledged its usefulness for instructors. 

In contrast, the negative and mixed comments focused on the limitations of the online compiler's functionality and its departure from industry-standard tools. A recurring concern was the lack of advanced debugging features, with several students expressing a preference for ``real IDEs.'' Others described the process reports as ``meaningless'' or ``felt coddled'' by the simplified environment.

\subsubsection{Students' Experience of Completing a Reflection Assignment}

Of the 59 students who participated in the survey, 50 had completed the reflection assignment. When asked whether the assignment helped their learning (Question 6), only 28 students ``agreed'' or ``strongly agreed,'' representing 56\% of those who completed it. This finding raises questions about \reviewchange{the value} of the end-of-semester reflection assignments \reviewchange{in their current format}, particularly when students are asked to complete them independently and all at once. However, when asked to share what they learned about their own coding habits (Question 7), they provided several insightful responses, including the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item ``I tried to do the projects before I fully understood the assignments.''
    \item ``I tend to brute-force code until it works.''
    \item ``I learned that planning in advance improves my code.''
    \item ``Taking breaks helped me avoid burnout and complete assignments.''
    \item ``I am too dependent on outside resources and don’t learn the language deeply.''
    \item ``I second guess myself and often rely on AI.''
    \item ``I started recognizing and correcting syntax/typo habits.''
    \item ``I realized I use too much AI and not enough problem-solving.''
    \item ``I learned that I need to slow down to avoid simple errors.''
\end{itemize}

Despite the relatively low reported helpfulness of the reflection assignment in its current format, these qualitative responses suggest that the act of reflection, even when prompted at the end of the course, \reviewchange{may increase} self-awareness of students' coding practices.

\section{Discussion}

Learning to code develops competencies beyond technical skills alone \cite{popat2019}. It positively influences educational outcomes, including the development of metacognitive abilities \cite{denny2024}. To fulfill these broader promises of coding education, process-oriented tools such as Process Feedback \reviewchange{may} engage students and support the development of thinking skills. For example, during moments of frustration while debugging, simply knowing how much time has been spent on a task can help students decide whether to continue troubleshooting or seek assistance.

Despite these benefits, not all students may be comfortable sharing their process data. In a separate study, the Process Feedback online compiler was demonstrated live to students without  requiring them to use it for their own coding. When asked about submitting their process reports to their instructors, some students expressed hesitation about sharing such detailed data \cite{arendes2024}. Similarly, in a study involving a different tool, approximately 10\% of the students expressed anxiety about plagiarism detection based on  keystroke data, when required to submit both their code and process data \cite{hart2023}. 

Students surveyed in this study, despite being required to use Process Feedback throughout the term and for every assignment, did not \reviewchange{raise} concerns about sharing their process data\reviewchange{ in their open-ended responses, although the survey did not ask about this directly}. This may be attributed to two key factors: (1) students were informed about the process-sharing requirement at the beginning of the course, and (2) they had several reasons to use the tool beyond merely demonstrating academic integrity. These findings suggest that if the sole purpose of tools such as ShowYourWork \cite{syndergaard2024, syndergaard_edwards2024}, InputLog \cite{leijten2013}, or Process Feedback \cite{adhikari2023} is to detect plagiarism, students may be reluctant to use them. Students need strong, pedagogically grounded reasons to use process-tracking tools. 

When students perceive these tools as resources to support their learning, improve their skills, collect data to share their struggles, acquire feedback on their process, or earn better grades, they \reviewchange{may be} more likely to adopt them willingly. Making it clear that the primary purpose is to support student learning should be considered a best practice \cite{yan2019}. In this spirit, placing students at the center of process-oriented learning by allowing them to view and reflect on their own process before sharing it with others can be effective. This stands in contrast to tools that collect behavioral data and provide insights to instructors that students themselves cannot access. 

How these tools are introduced also plays a critical role. For example, if an instructor demonstrates Process Feedback live in class and reflects on their own coding process, students may be more inclined to explore and use the tool themselves.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Integrating tools like Process Feedback into \reviewchange{an introductory programming} course \reviewchange{may discourage} plagiarism and provide instructors with valuable data to better understand students’ struggles and successes. However, the full potential of such tools is best realized when students are required to review their own coding process before sharing it with instructors. This practice of self-reflection \reviewchange{may encourage} students to critically examine their workflow and \reviewchange{support} the development of self-directed learning.

\reviewchange{This study relies on students' self-reported perceptions and does not measure learning gains, metacognitive growth, plagiarism, or instructor workload. The data come from a single institution and a single semester, and there was no comparison group or earlier cohort without the tool. The survey was voluntary, with a response rate of 53\%, so respondents may differ from students who did not respond, and the CS2 sample was small. In addition, two of the authors were involved in the development of Process Feedback, which may have influenced the design of the survey and the interpretation of the results.} Although \reviewchange{56\%} of surveyed students found the reflection assignment useful, the implementation likely limited its \reviewchange{value}. Students received no guidance on how to interpret their reports and were asked to reflect only once at the end of the term, reducing opportunities to apply insights to future work. Additionally, because the reflection was offered as a bonus assignment, \reviewchange{students who chose to complete it may differ from those who did not, and} students may have perceived it as low-stakes and allocated minimal time or effort. Only one reflection opportunity was provided; more frequent, smaller reflections distributed throughout the semester may have been more impactful. It is also possible that students were unclear about what constituted a meaningful reflection or what types of insights were expected.


\section{Conclusion}

This paper examined how online coding applications such as Process Feedback, which generate students' coding process reports, \reviewchange{were integrated into CS1 and CS2 courses}. It also described the approach of informing students in advance about the benefits of focusing on the process and asking them to engage in data-driven reflection on their coding process. Findings from a survey of 59 students indicate that the Process Feedback approach was positively received and perceived as beneficial to their learning experience. \reviewchange{The effects of this approach on learning and academic integrity remain to be tested in future studies.}
